Methodology

Data

Every MPC minutes publication since August 2015 — 96 documents — together with the Bank's voting record. Coverage is checked against the Bank's sitemap.

More detail

Text corpus

Every Monetary Policy Summary and minutes publication of the Bank of England's Monetary Policy Committee from August 2015 to September 2026: 96 documents — 94 from the regular meeting cycle plus 2 special (emergency) meetings, the 10 March and 19 March 2020 Covid-19 decisions. Corpus audit.

The window starts at the August 2015 regime change: from that meeting the Bank began publishing the policy summary and minutes on the day of the decision (“Super Thursday”). Meetings were monthly until autumn 2016, when the Bank moved to eight regular meetings a year. The first full year under that schedule was 2017. This corpus holds 11 documents for 2016 and 8 for 2017. The scraper probes every month and tolerates the months with no meeting rather than hardcoding the schedule. Bank meeting-schedule announcement; Collection code.

Until August 2021 the Bank published the full minutes as a separate PDF, with only the summary on the HTML page; 52 of the 96 documents were therefore reconstructed from the Bank's own PDF minutes, and every document records whether its text came from HTML or PDF. PDF extraction code.

The corpus was reconciled against the Bank's own sitemap (zero missing, zero extra within the window) and against the voting record. A document's published date is meeting_end + 1 day, applied uniformly; the five known one-day gaps against the Bank's voting sheet are individually logged, and joins between the two sources use a ±3-day tolerance. Date reconciliation.

Raw source texts are © Bank of England and stay in a local, gitignored cache; the repository publishes derived scores and SHA-256 hashes only, with links to the source pages. Published index data.

Voting record

The Bank's published MPC voting spreadsheet, parsed to one row per (meeting, member): each member's preferred Bank Rate against the decided rate. skew is the average of all voting members' preferred rates minus the decided rate: positive means members leaned towards higher rates than the collective decision. The construction follows Apel & Blix Grimaldi (2012), drawing on Gerlach-Kristen (2004). The sheet's date column is the announcement date, confirmed against known meetings. Voting-record code.

Why the sample sizes differ

Each test uses a different set of meetings. The 96 documents cover every MPC minutes publication from August 2015 to September 2026, including the two emergency meetings of March 2020.

  • 64 evaluated. The benchmark ladder scores only meetings from 1 January 2019 onwards; everything earlier is training data for the expanding window and is never scored. That leaves 64 meetings inside the evaluation window.
  • 62 scheduled. Of those 64, the 2 March 2020 emergency meetings are scored separately rather than blended in, because no call could have been locked for a meeting that had not yet been announced. The headline ladder figures are the remaining 62.
  • 93 in Spec 3. The regression uses the whole corpus rather than the 2019 window, and its dependent variable is the rate surprise. That drops the 2 specials, and August 2015 as well — the first document in the corpus has no preceding decision to measure a change from. 96 − 2 − 1 = 93.
  • 25 in the fragility check. The post-hiking-cycle subsample is those of the 93 falling on or after 1 September 2023.

The forecast comparison reserves earlier meetings for training. The regression instead uses the longer history to estimate a relationship. Forecast results; Statistical results.

Index construction

Each document is scored with a published 2012 dictionary of hawkish and dovish word pairs, producing one number on a 0–2 scale where 1.0 is neutral.

Why this dictionary. Its word lists were published before this project and are not fitted to these minutes. That makes the method reproducible without choosing words to suit the result. This adapts the original Swedish study to English-language MPC minutes; it does not replicate that study in full. The lists are the paper's 2012 vintage. A match such as “higher inflation” records the topic discussed rather than the stance taken; and hits are sparse, a median of 2 per document. A new dictionary developed after seeing these results would be exploratory on this history; untouched future calls would be needed to test it prospectively. Dictionary choice.

More detail

The index follows Apel & Blix Grimaldi (2012). The original word lists and page references are in the stored dictionary.

Net Index = [ (#hawk / (#hawk + #dove)) − (#dove / (#hawk + #dove)) ] + 1   Original formula

Range [0, 2]; 1.0 is neutral (the ratio's fixed midpoint, not a fitted value). A document with zero hits is defined as neutral. Scoring code.

Counting rule, per the paper: the whole document is lowercased and tokenised (word tokens; no sentence splitting — the paper counts across the full text). Each adjacent adjective-then-noun pair (“higher inflation”) counts as one hawkish or dovish hit if the adjective matches one of the paper's stems by prefix; multi-word nouns (“oil price”, “cyclical position”) match as a following token sequence. The seven core nouns of the paper's main Net Index are used; the paper's four-noun robustness extension is stored but unused. Immediate adjacency in the adjective-then-noun direction is an interpretation of the paper's worked examples, logged as such. There is no negation handling — the paper has none. Matching rules.

Lag convention: a meeting's minutes are published simultaneously with its own decision, so in every predictive use (the benchmark ladder's L3 and the Spec 2/Spec 3 tests) the tone feature for a meeting is the previous meeting's index. Forecast specification.

Market benchmark

The benchmark approximates market expectations using the Bank's OIS forward curve and SONIA, the overnight interest rate. Their difference is mapped into cut, hold and hike probabilities. Historical reads use the first one-month curve point, not a rate specific to the meeting.

More detail

Sources. The Bank of England's published instantaneous OIS forward curve (the daily archive oisddata.zip, whose era files cover 2009–2015 / 2016–2024 / 2025–present, plus the separate “latest yield curve data” file updated daily — whichever is fresher wins), and SONIA from the Bank's Interactive Statistical Database (series IUDSOIA). Market-data code.

Implied change. Subtract SONIA from a forward read requested at meeting date + 3 days. The code interpolates only within the available maturity range and clips to an endpoint outside it. Every published historical row requests a five-day maturity, below the first one-month node, so every row uses that node. The offset has no effect on these historical reads. This is an OIS-based proxy, not a meeting-specific probability distribution; the fitted curve can mix pricing across neighbouring meetings. Numerical v1 results are retained. Forward-rate mapping; Benchmark correction.

Two-state ±25bp mapping. The market is assumed to price a single possible move in one direction, of fixed 25bp size: phike or pcut = clip(|implied change| / 25, 0, 1) in the direction of the implied change, remainder phold. A documented simplification — real market pricing can imply multi-directional or non-25bp moves. Move-size assumption.

Historical benchmark. For each of the 96 meetings, the curve valuation date is announcement − 2 calendar days, walked back day-by-day to the nearest available trading day (capped at 10 days, else a hard stop; in practice zero walk-backs occurred). Historical market data; Historical lookup code.

Valuation date is not availability. This historical backtest does not reconstruct timestamped public releases. The Bank aims to publish curves by noon on the following business day, and archive data may be revised. SONIA is published on the next London business day, with another working-day delay for the free database used here. Therefore a row's lock_date does not establish that its inputs could have been downloaded then. The live locks retain their actual fetched inputs. A release-aware historical benchmark requires a separate version, not a silent replacement of v1. Curve publication policy; SONIA publication policy. Availability limitation.

Freshness guard. A live lock refuses to write if the curve snapshot is more than 2 business days old. Lock checks.

Evaluation

Does the extra information lead to better predictions? The models are fitted only on earlier meetings, then scored on the next decision. The market proxy is the benchmark. Lower probability errors mean better forecasts.

Two different questions. The forecast comparison tests accuracy on meetings outside the training sample. The statistical tests below look for relationships within the historical sample. A relationship can exist without being strong or stable enough to improve a forecast.

More detail

Benchmark ladder (L0–L4)

  • L0 — always hold (phold = 1), the naive baseline.
  • L1 — market-only: the two-state ±25bp mapping above, exactly as in the historical market data. This is the benchmark.
  • L2 — ordered logit (proportional odds, 3 classes) on the market-implied change alone. It learns how earlier market pricing relates to cut, hold and hike decisions.
  • L3 — ordered logit on the market-implied change plus the previous meeting's tone index and vote skew (both lagged; see the lag convention above).
  • L4 — member-level simulation: a pooled dissent-state transition matrix (fit on strictly prior votes) blended 0.5/0.5 with the market-implied distribution per member, aggregated over 5,000 Monte Carlo committee draws (fixed seed 42).

Expanding window: for each evaluated meeting, models are fit on strictly prior meetings only, training from August 2015; evaluation runs from 1 January 2019 (64 meetings). Outcomes are coded by sign only — {cut, hold, hike} — with one shared classifier (the verb adjacent to “Bank Rate” in the decision text). An expanding window means the training history grows after each meeting; the model never trains on the decision it is about to predict. Comparison code; Decision classifier.

Scheduled-only rule. Headline scores use the 62 scheduled meetings only: at lock time (2 business days before an announcement) nobody could have pre-registered a forecast for an emergency meeting not yet known to exist. The 2 specials are scored separately as a robustness line, never blended in. Scheduled-meeting rule.

Scoring rules. These score the whole probability distribution, not just whether the most likely outcome happened. The Brier score adds up the squared probability errors; the log score penalises confident mistakes especially heavily. Both are lower-is-better.

Three-class Brier score (sum of squared probability errors across {cut, hold, hike}; range [0, 2]) and log score −log p(actual outcome), with p floored at the clip constant 1e−9 to avoid −log(0) for a confidently wrong call; the clip is published in the forecast results as log_score_probability_clip. Scoring code; Published scores.

Fallbacks. An ordered-logit window with under 10 training examples, fewer than 2 outcome classes, or a non-converged fit (two-part check, including a degenerate-parameter guard) falls back to L1's probabilities and is recorded in the forecast results under fallback_log — 11 of 64 L3 windows, all before the sample's first cut in March 2020. Model safeguards.

Statistical tests (Spec 2 and Spec 3)

Does tone line up with rate surprises? (Spec 3)
Subtract the market proxy's expected change from the actual rate change. Fit a line relating that surprise to the previous minutes' tone. This is ordinary least squares (OLS). The slope, called the coefficient, measures the average difference in surprise associated with a unit increase in the tone index.
Does tone help explain cut, hold or hike? (Spec 2)
An ordered-logit model assigns probabilities to those ordered outcomes. The likelihood-ratio test compares a market-only model with one that also uses tone. It asks whether tone improves historical fit, not whether it improves forecasts on unseen meetings.
How uncertain is the estimate?
Newey–West standard errors allow the regression's unexplained movements to vary in size and remain related across nearby meetings. The t-statistic measures the slope's distance from zero relative to its estimated uncertainty. A p-value asks how often a test statistic at least this extreme would arise if there were no relationship, under the model's assumptions. It is not the probability that the finding is true.

Technical specification

Spec 3: OLS of the market surprise (actual minus market-implied rate change, basis points; surprise data) on the lagged tone index, Newey–West HAC standard errors with 4 lags, on the 93 scheduled meetings; also run with lagged skew added, and on a post-hiking-cycle fragility subsample (from 1 September 2023, n=25). Spec 2: a likelihood-ratio test of whether adding the lagged index improves an ordered logit of the three-class decision over the market-implied change alone, with a minimum-sample guard of 10 observations. Statistical-test code; Full statistical results.

Lock-and-score protocol

Before an announcement, the locking code freezes the market-only reference and the current index readings with the git commit hash and SHA-256 hashes of every input used; a human then fills in the point call and rationale, and the committed prediction file is the pre-registered record — never modified afterwards, misses included. Locks refuse stale curve data (see the freshness guard above). Lock-and-score protocol.

Limitations

What could undermine the result: very few dictionary hits per document, a 2012 word list, risk premia inside the forward curve, a small sample, and a skill differential that has not been tested for significance.

More detail
  • Sparse lexicon hits. Across the 96 documents, the median number of A&BG bigram hits per document (hawkish + dovish) is 2, with an interquartile range of 2 (Q1 = 1, Q3 = 3; min 0, max 12) — each document's index value rests on very few matches, and a single hit either way moves the number substantially. Quartiles use the median-inclusive convention. Dictionary-hit analysis.
  • Risk premia in forwards. OIS forward rates embed risk/term premia as well as rate expectations, so the market-implied probabilities are not pure forecasts. Market benchmark.
  • First-node clipping and release timing. Historical reads use the first one-month node, not a meeting-specific forward. Smoothing can mix neighbouring meetings; the offset does not fix this in the published backtest. Archive valuation dates do not verify public availability or preserve unrevised vintages. These limitations qualify every comparison with L1. Curve and release-timing assumptions.
  • Lexicon vintage and topic-vs-stance. The term lists are the paper's 2012 vintage, used unmodified; a match such as “higher inflation” reflects what the Committee discussed, which need not be the stance it took. Dictionary construction.
  • No negation handling. Per the paper, a phrase like “not higher inflation” still counts its inner bigram; the paper's method has no negation rule and none was added. Matching rules.
  • What the models identify. L3 adds tone and vote skew together, so its result does not isolate tone's incremental contribution. L4 uses votes, not tone. Its majority state maps to hold even when the majority voted to move; this is a v1 modelling assumption, not evidence that votes have no information. Target-meeting rosters come from the voting sheet, and their availability before each historical forecast has not been established.
  • Retrospective design. A new dictionary inspired by these episodes would be exploratory on this historical sample, even with an expanding window. Untouched future locked outcomes are needed to assess it prospectively.
  • Small n. 96 documents in total; 62 scheduled meetings in the ladder's evaluation window; 25 in the fragility subsample. Sample definitions.
  • No significance test on the skill differential. Skill against L1 is a point value. Whether L3's negative skill is distinguishable from zero on 62 meetings has not been tested. A Diebold–Mariano test on the Brier loss differential (L1 against L2, L3 and L4) was proposed in the log but is not present in the published results. Any new test must be registered and versioned before it is run. Proposed forecast-comparison test.
  • No standard errors published. The statistical results carry each coefficient's estimate, t-statistic and p-value but not its standard error. They are not reconstructed from already-rounded numbers here. A later result version should publish uncertainty directly from the fitted model. Published uncertainty measures.
  • The two specifications disagree. Spec 3 (OLS on the continuous surprise) is nominally significant on the full sample (p = 0.0375) but is not statistically distinguishable from zero in the smaller later sample (p = 0.1777). The later estimate has the same sign; these overlapping samples are not an independent replication test. Spec 2 (ordered logit on the discrete decision) finds no statistically significant improvement (p = 0.4997). Both are reported. Both statistical tests.

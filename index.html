About this project

I'm Jake Foulkes, an MPhil in Economics student at the University of Oxford. Before each Bank of England rate decision I publish a forecast with a timestamp, and I score it afterwards, misses included.

The project also asks whether the language of the Monetary Policy Committee's minutes adds anything to what financial markets already expect.

The comparison. L0 always predicts hold. L1 uses market pricing directly. L2 fits a model using market pricing alone. L3 also uses the previous minutes' tone and the balance of dissenting votes (“skew”). L4 gives equal weight to market pricing and patterns in earlier votes, conditional on each member's previous voting position, then simulates committee decisions. Full model definitions.

Out of sample. At each meeting, the training sample expands to include earlier meetings, never the meeting being predicted. Emergency meetings are reported separately: a call could not have been pre-registered for an unannounced meeting.

Why the sample sizes differ. The corpus is 96 documents (August 2015 – September 2026, including the two emergency meetings of March 2020). The ladder evaluates from 1 January 2019 onwards, leaving everything earlier as training data only — 64 meetings, of which the 62 scheduled ones carry the headline scores above and the 2 specials are scored separately. Read more: Brier, log score and skill formulas

For each outcome (cut, hold, hike), let p be its forecast probability and y indicate whether it happened: one for the actual outcome, zero for the others.

Brier = sum of (p − y)² across outcomes

A perfect forecast has no squared error. The table averages the score over meetings. This scores the whole distribution, so two forecasts with the same most likely outcome can earn different scores.

Log score = −ln(probability assigned to the actual outcome)

The log score penalises very confident mistakes especially heavily. To keep it finite, the calculation uses a small probability floor, 1e−9.

Skill = one − (model's mean Brier / market's mean Brier)

Zero is a tie with the market, positive is better, negative is worse. Full evaluation specification.

Is tone linked to the unexpected part of a decision?

A market surprise is the actual rate change minus the change implied by the market proxy. The full-history test finds an association with the previous minutes' tone. The estimate has the same sign but is more uncertain in the smaller sample from 1 September 2023. A separate test finds no statistically significant improvement in explaining cut, hold or hike.

A relationship is not a better forecast. These tests look for patterns in the historical sample. The forecast comparison above asks a harder question: did the models improve predictions for meetings they were not trained on? They did not. Neither exercise establishes that tone causes a rate decision.

Read more: the regression, p-values and robustness checks
Regression (Spec 3)
Fits a line through previous tone readings and subsequent rate surprises. The coefficient is the line's slope: how much the surprise changes, in basis points, for a unit increase in tone. A negative slope means a more hawkish reading is associated with a lower surprise.
Decision model (Spec 2)
Ordered logit assigns probabilities to the ordered outcomes cut, hold and hike. A likelihood-ratio test asks whether adding tone improves the fit over market pricing alone. This is a test of historical fit, not forecast accuracy.
p-value
If there were no relationship, how often would a test statistic at least this extreme arise, given the model's assumptions? A smaller value is stronger evidence against no relationship, not the probability that the finding is true.
Newey–West standard errors
Allow for unexplained movements that vary in size or persist across nearby meetings. They adjust the uncertainty around the slope, not the slope itself.

The later sample is smaller and overlaps the full sample. Its greater uncertainty does not establish that the relationship changed, and it is not an independent replication.

Regressing each meeting's market surprise on the previous meeting's index (OLS, Newey–West standard errors, 4 lags, n=93 scheduled meetings): coefficient −2.08 (t = −2.08, p = 0.0375). On the post-hiking-cycle subsample (from 1 September 2023, n=25) the estimate has the same sign but is not statistically distinguishable from zero: coefficient −2.97 (t = −1.35, p = 0.1777). Spec 2, an ordered-logit likelihood-ratio test on the discrete decision, finds no statistically significant improvement: LR = 0.4556, p = 0.4997. Coefficients and t-statistics are rounded to 2 decimal places and p-values to 4; full precision is in the full statistical results.

No standard errors are published. The statistical results carry each coefficient's estimate, t-statistic and p-value but not its standard error. They are not reconstructed from rounded figures here. See Limitations.

My probability of a hike is 89.55%, the same as m0, with 10.45% on hold and zero on a cut. This is the first call where I have not shaded the market probability. The communication index stays out of the call: L3 scores −0.6250 against the market-only benchmark and Spec 2 returns p = 0.4997. The index reads 1.333 against a trailing four-document mean of 1.179, hawkish and pointing the same way as the curve, but the project has not shown that using it improves the forecast. September’s reason for shading down does not carry over. The meeting-plus-three read still clips to the first one-month node, but that node now falls around 29 November, after this decision and before December’s. A November hike belongs in that price. The first four forwards are 3.9587%, 4.0463%, 4.1504% and 4.2657%. Smoothing may still pull some later pricing into the first point, but I cannot use those nodes to put a number on the effect. September’s correct hold did not validate the mechanism. I have no sufficiently well-supported reason to replace the curve’s probability with my own. The data since September gives the four conditional hawks more reason to move. September CPI rose to 3.5% from 3.1%, led by motor fuels, but core also rose to 2.8% from 2.6% and services to 3.7% from 3.4%. In September I argued that the headline had moved while the measures the majority was waiting for had stayed still. That is harder to argue now. The October pay release put regular earnings growth at 3.7%, with private-sector growth edging up to 3.0% after three periods at 2.9%, though those figures cover June–August and predate the September meeting. The more recent business survey found 68% of firms expecting to raise prices in response to the energy shock, eleven percentage points more than in September. Expected wage growth, however, remained at 3.4%. The evidence is stronger on prices than on wages. My judgement is that the four will now regard the risk of waiting as greater than the risk of moving before the wage evidence is conclusive. The vote split is called at 7–2: Bailey, Lombardelli, Breeden and Ramsden join Greene, Mann and Pill, with Dhingra and Taylor holding. Against that, vacancies fell by 21,000 to 691,000 and firms’ wage expectations have not risen. There is still a case for waiting, and I would reconsider if the central bloc made clear that sustained wage acceleration remained a condition for a rise. My hold alternative is 5–4, with Lombardelli crossing first and Bailey, Breeden and Ramsden waiting. A hold would mean I had mistaken their willingness to discuss a rise for a willingness to vote for one. A 5–4 hike would get the decision right but show that I had expected too many of them to move together.

How the test works

  1. Measure the language. Count phrases from a fixed dictionary. Hawkish language points towards tighter policy, such as higher rates; dovish language points towards easier policy, such as lower rates.
  2. Start with the market. The overnight index swap (OIS) curve reflects pricing of future overnight interest rates. Compare a forward with SONIA to construct a simplified cut, hold or hike proxy. Historical reads use the first one-month node, not a meeting-specific price.
  3. Ask whether tone adds anything. For each historical forecast, fit the models on earlier meetings only. Add the previous meeting's tone and voting balance, then compare their forecasts with the market benchmark.
  4. Score the probabilities. A forecast earns a better score when it assigns more probability to what happens. Confident mistakes cost more than cautious ones.

The timing matters: the minutes published with a decision can help forecast the following meeting, but cannot be used to predict the decision they were published alongside.

Read more: the index formula and what it misses

Let H be the number of hawkish dictionary matches and D the number of dovish matches. The index compares their shares:

Index = (H − D) / (H + D) + neutral midpoint

The neutral midpoint is one. The scale runs from zero (only dovish matches) to two (only hawkish matches). Equal counts give a neutral reading; a document with no matches is also assigned neutral.

The dictionary matches adjacent adjective-and-noun phrases, such as “higher inflation”. It does not interpret whole sentences, negation or whether a risk is conditional. A neutral score can mean balanced language or simply no recognised phrases. The median document has just 2 matches, so a few phrases can move the reading substantially. Index construction.

Read more: turning the OIS curve into probabilities

Subtract SONIA from the selected OIS forward. The requested post-meeting date lies below the first one-month node in every historical row, so the code uses that node. Express the difference in basis points. A basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. Assume the possible move is 25 basis points in the direction of the difference:

Probability of a move = |implied change| / assumed move size

Keep that probability between zero and one; the remaining probability goes to hold. A positive difference gives hike-or-hold; a negative difference gives cut-or-hold. This assumption cannot represent simultaneous chances of a cut and a hike, or several possible move sizes.

The curve is smoothed and can blur pricing across neighbouring meetings. It can also include compensation for risk. Historical archives do not establish public availability at the stated lock date. These are proxy probabilities, not an exact reconstruction of tradable meeting pricing. Market benchmark and assumptions.

Read more: what this project contributes

A published dictionary (Apel & Blix Grimaldi, 2012) is applied to Bank of England minutes and judged against market expectations. Predicting a decision correctly is less informative when the market already expected it.

Alongside the historical test, each live call is published, timestamped and locked before the announcement. The outcome is scored afterwards; misses stay on the record. The meeting notes examine what the dictionary captures and misses, including conditional language about future inflation risks.

Glossary

Read more: the main terms in plain English
Brier score
The squared error of a probability forecast, summed over the possible outcomes — 0 is a perfect call, and lower is better.
Log score
Minus the logarithm of the probability the forecast gave to whatever actually happened — lower is better, and it punishes confident mistakes far harder than Brier does.
Skill score
How much a model improves on a benchmark: 0 is a tie with the benchmark, positive is better than it, negative is worse.
OIS curve
Overnight index swap curve — the rate at which the market will today fix a future overnight interest rate, and so a reading of where it expects Bank Rate to go.
SONIA
The Sterling Overnight Index Average: the actual overnight rate at which banks lend, and the level the OIS curve is measured against.
Ordered logit
A regression for outcomes with a natural order — cut, hold, hike — that returns a probability for each rather than a single prediction.
Pre-registration
Publishing a forecast, timestamped, before the event it is about, so that it cannot be quietly revised once the answer is known.

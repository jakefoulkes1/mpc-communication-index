The lexicon caught it anyway

5 October 2026

The September call scored cleanly. Bank Rate held at 3.75%, the locked probabilities beat the market, and the vote split, scored for the first time rather than carried as an ancillary, came in where I had put most of the weight. None of that is what this note is about. In July I made a prediction about the instrument rather than the Committee, and September broke it. That is the more useful result, and it is the subject here.

1. What happened

At the meeting ending 16 September, announced on the 17th, the MPC voted six to three to maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%. Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill again preferred a quarter-point rise to 4%. It was the sixth consecutive meeting at 3.75%. After three meetings in which the minority had grown by one each time, it held at three.

The vote stood still; almost nothing around it did. Brent crude and UK wholesale gas had risen by 36% and 78% since the run-up to the July Report, and the staff projection now put CPI near 3.75% in the fourth quarter, against 3.2% in July, and slightly above 4% early in 2027. The Federal Reserve had raised its target range the day before, taking its top above Bank Rate for the first time in this cycle. The Committee judged the risks to inflation tilted further to the upside than at its previous meeting.

The movement was inside the six. The minutes now split them. Dhingra and Taylor put particular weight on slack, on restrained pass-through of costs to prices and on the restrictive level of Bank Rate, all of which in their view allowed more time to observe. The other four wrote like people preparing to move. Bailey said that if the conflict persisted and the risk of second-round effects grew, policy would likely have to tighten. Lombardelli said the case for raising was building the longer the conflict went unresolved. Breeden said a response became increasingly appropriate if the risks crystallised. Ramsden said there could be a case for a rise if upside pressures continued to build. And the Committee as a whole adopted a line that in July had belonged to the minority: given the lags with which second-round effects appear, it was "not appropriate to wait too long for evidence" before responding. In vote space the Committee is six to three. In argument it is closer to three, four and two.

The dissenters, meanwhile, converged. In July they gave three different reasons: pre-emption, geopolitics, signalling. In September the minutes present a shared case. Inflation will peak in early 2027 just as wage settlements are agreed; the slack that was supposed to contain second-round effects appears already to have peaked; and under that uncertainty, risk management is the right frame.

2. What I called

Hold at 3.75%. Correct. The probabilities were locked on 16 September with p(hike) at 20%, shaded below the market-only 28.9% on the first of July's two reasons only, the one-month clip. They scored a Brier of 0.0800 against the market's 0.1673. The shade ran against the intelligence of the moment: the minutes record that in the days before the meeting the perceived probability of a near-term rise had gone up. It paid here. That is one outcome, and the reason behind it is still to be tested against the November lock.

The vote split was pre-registered as a scored distribution, as July said it should be, with half the weight on three dissents. Brier 0.3400. The lock named Greene, Mann and Pill, and those were the three. Its stated failure condition, a hike or a fourth vote for one, did not occur, so the reading of the central bloc's threshold held for this meeting. The minutes suggest it will not hold for long. Four of the six now describe that threshold as approaching. That is November's question, not September's credit.

3. Why I was wrong

The communication index was recorded as context and kept out of the call. But the July episode made a prediction about it, and the September lock repeated it: a lexicon keyed to realised-inflation language would read the Committee as dovish while the hawkish dissent held, because the dissent rested on forward risk the dictionary cannot see. The July minutes bore that out, at 0.6667. The September minutes read 1.3333, hawkish, above the 1.2203 mean of the four documents before them. The dissent held. The prediction failed.

The inspector output shows how. The index counts adjacent adjective–noun pairs across the whole text; it never sees a sentence. Of its four hawkish hits in September, three sit inside conditional, forward-looking sentences: that reactions to higher energy prices "might feed into higher wage settlements", that above-target inflation running into a wage round "could increase inflationary pressures", and that buffers would only delay costs passing "into higher prices if the conflict persisted". None of these is a claim about realised inflation. The first is chiefly a statement that the Committee did not know. The pairs fired, higher wage, increase inflationary, higher prices, and the modal two words to the left was invisible. The fourth hawkish hit sits in the sentence restating July's judgement that the risk of strong inflationary pressures outweighed the risk of weak ones, and it fires alongside one of the two dovish hits. The Committee's plainest statement of where it thought the risk lay scores as zero.

So the wrong-axis claim narrows rather than dies. The lexicon is blind to forward-risk reasoning as such, not to forward-risk reasoning whenever it appears. It catches that reasoning when it happens to be phrased in inflation vocabulary, and misses it when it arrives as insurance, anchoring, signalling or geopolitics. Whether the index registers the dimension that moves votes depends on the Committee's phrasing, not its reasoning.

Where the hits sit matters too. All three conditional sentences are in the Committee's collective paragraphs, not the members' own statements, and the first section is the reason. In July forward-risk reasoning was the minority's argument. By September the majority had absorbed it, and the collective prose expresses it in the Bank's house vocabulary of wages and prices. The index rose because the argument spread, not because the vote moved.

Seen that way, the deeper July claim survives. The index and the vote did diverge; they diverged the other way. In July the index fell while the minority grew. In September the index rose while the minority held. I got the sign wrong because I assumed the lexicon would stay blind, when what it sees depends on who is writing and in which words.

One caveat travels with all of it. The reading rests on six hits in total, and two fewer hawkish ones would read neutral. One thin document illustrates a mechanism. It does not measure one.

4. What it changes

Two things, of different orders.

The smaller is a reminder about the ladder. One meeting, taking the sample from 61 scheduled meetings to 62, moved L3 skill from −0.6712 to −0.6250: a single document shifted the headline score by nearly five hundredths. Spec 3 still holds at p = 0.0375, and its skew-controlled variant, at 0.0477, is one meeting from losing significance at 5%. Neither movement is a trend, and neither should be read as one.

The larger is that the risk-management series now has to be specified against September. It must identify forward-risk reasoning by its construction rather than its vocabulary: conditionals, modals attached to risk, pre-emption, insurance, arguments about how long to wait for evidence. It must state in advance what happens to a sentence that scores in both series, because September shows the overlap is real: the sentences that should drive the new series already drive the old one. Left undefined, that overlap would manufacture or cancel by construction the very divergence the series exists to test. And it should decide in advance whether collective paragraphs and members' statements are scored separately, since September suggests forward-risk language migrates from one to the other as a view spreads. The protocol is unchanged: pre-registered before the November lock, run over the full sample from January 2019 under the expanding window, and nothing fitted to September.

One discipline note, because it is the whole point of the exercise. Two locked calls, both correct, is n = 2. The shade below the market has paid twice, and that says nothing yet about calibration; the always-hold reference, which no one would publish as a forecast, scored perfectly at both meetings. The prediction I lost taught more than either call I won, and it cost nothing in the ledger, which is what July said of the unscored vote split. If the index is going to make predictions, they should be locked with the call.

The minority grew

30 July 2026

The July call scored cleanly. Bank Rate held at 3.75% on 30 July; the locked probabilities returned a Brier of 0.003; nothing in the record moved between lock and score but the outcome field and the scores that follow from it. That is the mechanism working exactly as designed, and it is not what this note is about. The interesting thing about July is not the call. It is the part of the reading that failed while the call succeeded.

1. What happened

At the meeting ending 29 July, announced on the 30th, the MPC voted six to three to maintain Bank Rate at 3.75%. Megan Greene, Catherine Mann and Huw Pill preferred an immediate quarter-point rise to 4.00%. It was the fifth consecutive meeting at 3.75% and the third consecutive meeting at which the hawkish minority has grown: 8–1 in April, 7–2 in June, 6–3 in July. The run begins from a unanimous 9–0 in March — the Committee's first undivided decision in four and a half years — which makes the sequence cleaner still: one dissent added at each of three successive meetings from a base of none.

The accompanying Monetary Policy Report carried a central projection of CPI peaking near 3.2% in the fourth quarter. At the press conference Bailey moved directly to damp the hawkish reading, telling reporters not to leave the room thinking the Bank was edging towards a hike. On the day, then, the vote record and the Governor's communication pointed in opposite directions, and the Governor was managing that divergence deliberately.

2. What I called

Hold at 3.75%. Correct. The probabilities were locked on 28 July under the announcement-minus-two convention, with p(hike) shaded modestly below the market-implied 3.86% for the two documented regime-specific reasons: a forward read at meeting-plus-three, interpolated across whole-month buckets on a steeply upward-sloping short curve, absorbs September's expectations into July's; and the fiscal repricing of 20 July that carried the ten-year gilt to 5.04% is a risk premium rather than an expectation of Bank Rate. Both remain testable against the September lock, when the near-month slope will differ. The communication index was recorded as context and kept out of the call, on the standing ground that L3 scores −0.6729 against the market-only benchmark and Spec 2 returns p = 0.4918. [Figures as at the 28 July lock. The ladder was re-run on 10 August after the July minutes were ingested and now reads −0.6712 / 0.4894 — Ed., September 2026.] There is no warrant on this project's own evidence for letting tone override L1, and July gave none.

The ancillary vote-split call — unscored — was that the minority would hold at two. It went to three. Mann moved. She was named in the lock-day rationale as the member most likely to reason her way towards an activist hike while continuing to vote for a hold. I had identified the right person and drawn precisely the wrong conclusion about her.

3. Why I was wrong

The argument was that the macro flow since 18 June ran against the two dissenters on nearly every measure they had invoked. Headline CPI fell to 2.6% against a 2.7% consensus, its lowest in fifteen months; services eased to 3.6%; regular pay growth slowed to 3.4%, joint-slowest since October 2020; input producer prices decelerated to 7.3% from 9.3% as Brent retraced. Greene and Pill, on that reading, had dissented on an energy shock that was already unwinding.

The flow did run against them. Every one of those figures held. They dissented anyway, and a third member joined them.

So the error is not in the data. It is in the assumption that connected the data to the votes. I had read the June dissents as claims about realised inflation — that prices were running hotter than the majority allowed — and inferred that a run of disinflationary prints would erode the claim and, with it, the dissent. That is not what the dissenters were saying, and July made it explicit. Their stated reasons, taken together:

  • Greene argued that moving early reduces the probability that second-round effects take hold at all.
  • Mann named the decisive change as diplomatic and geopolitical — the collapse of the US–Iran memorandum, the widening of the conflict, the energy volatility that followed — and not any price series.
  • Pill argued for raising now precisely in order to cut through the noise in commodity and asset prices, as an unambiguous signal of willingness to act on upside risk.

Not one of the three is an argument from realised inflation. Pill's is the sharpest against me: he treats the incoming data as noise to be seen through. My reasoning assigned decisive weight to a series that the decisive voters had explicitly set aside.

Bailey's own framing is the gap in a sentence. Inflation has fallen faster than expected, to 2.6%; the Bank's central projection has it peaking near 3.2% in Q4. Both are true at once. A committee looking backwards sees disinflation; a committee looking forwards sees a peak still ahead of it. The minority is not disputing the first fact. It is pricing the second. They are not reasoning from realised disinflation. They are reasoning from forward risk.

4. What it changes

Two things, of different orders.

The smaller is a correction to how I read the Committee. It is not divided about the object of concern. Bailey, Breeden, Lombardelli, Ramsden, Taylor and Dhingra all framed the hold in terms of second-round effects, insurance, or the conditions under which they would tighten; Ramsden said outright that crystallised upside risk could warrant a hike and that subsiding risk would have him considering a resumption of cuts. Everyone is watching the same thing. What separates the six from the three is the evidentiary threshold for acting on it before it appears. Greene would move pre-emptively; Ramsden would move on confirmation. On a hawk–dove scale those two positions read as near-neighbours. In vote space they are opposite.

The larger is that this may be a problem with what the index measures rather than with how I read its output. A lexicon keyed to realised-inflation language is not mis-calibrated here; it is oriented along the wrong axis. It cannot see the dimension the July vote actually turned on, and its failure mode is worse than silence: it will score July's disinflationary language as dovish at exactly the meeting where the hawkish minority grew. The index and the vote record will diverge systematically, and the divergence will present as noise rather than as the finding it is.

That points to a second series rather than a rescaling of the first — a risk-management measure counting conditionality, pre-emption, insurance, second-round effects, signalling and tail risk, scored separately from realised-inflation tone. The testable proposition is that the gap between the two carries the vote information: that in July the realised-inflation series fell while the risk-management series rose, and that the divergence, not the level of either, is what tracks dissent counts.

One discipline note, because it is the whole point of the exercise. None of this touches the July record, and none of it may be fitted to July. If a second series is built, it must be specified and pre-registered before the September lock and run over the full sample from January 2019 under the same expanding-window protocol as everything else. Otherwise the fix is in-sample, and the negative result it claims to overturn is merely reproduced with extra steps.

The last change I am persuaded of is procedural. The vote split should be pre-registered as a scored distribution from September rather than carried as an unscored ancillary. July's miss cost nothing in the ledger — which is precisely the problem. A reading that can be wrong without penalty is not doing the work the rest of the project does.
